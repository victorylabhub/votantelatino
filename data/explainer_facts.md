# Verified facts: U.S. general election, Nov. 3, 2026

## 1. What is on the ballot nationally

- All **435 U.S. House seats** are up. Five of the six non-voting delegates (D.C. and inhabited territories) are also on the ballot.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections (secondary; 435 is the fixed House size: https://www.house.gov/representatives)
- **35 U.S. Senate seats** are up: **33** regular (Class 2) seats, plus **2 special elections**, in **Florida** (remainder of Marco Rubio's term) and **Ohio** (remainder of JD Vance's term).
  Sources: https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/03/09/both-gubernatorial-and-u-s-senate-elections-are-happening-in-26-states-in-2026/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_Senate_elections
- **36 governor races** are on the ballot in 2026. In **26 states**, voters choose both a governor and a U.S. senator.
  Source: https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/03/09/both-gubernatorial-and-u-s-senate-elections-are-happening-in-26-states-in-2026/
- **State legislatures:** About **6,139 of 7,386** state legislative seats (83%) are up. Races are in **88 of 99 chambers**, in **46 states**. Louisiana, Mississippi, New Jersey and Virginia hold no legislative races in 2026 because they hold state elections in odd-numbered years.
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/2026-legislative-races-by-state-and-chamber
- **Statewide ballot measures:** **164** were certified for 2026 in **40 states**. **145** of them are on the Nov. 3 ballot. Voters will also see local races (county, city, school board, judges) and local measures, which differ by place.
  Source: https://ballotpedia.org/2026_ballot_measures (no explicit as-of date; the latest timeline entry is Sept. 4, 2026)
- **[NOTE]** Several states redrew their congressional maps in the middle of the decade (2025–2026), so some voters may be in a different House district than in 2024. A secondary source lists new maps in AL, CA, FL, LA, NC, OH, TN, TX and UT, and says Missouri's 2022 map stays in effect pending a referendum. **[UNCONFIRMED against official sources; verify per state before publishing.]**
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections

## 2. Same-day / Election Day voter registration

- NCSL: **24 states plus D.C.** offer same-day registration. That count leaves out Alaska and Rhode Island, which allow it only for president and vice president (not relevant in 2026). Page updated March 27, 2026.
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/same-day-voter-registration
- **You can register on Election Day itself (Nov. 3)** in these 21 states plus D.C.: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Iowa, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Mexico, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, Wisconsin, Wyoming, and the District of Columbia.
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/same-day-voter-registration
- **Same-day registration during early voting only (NOT on Election Day):** Massachusetts, New York, North Carolina.
  Source: same as above
- **Montana:** late registration runs until **noon the day before** the election, so there is no Election Day registration.
  Source: same as above
- **North Dakota** has no voter registration. **[NOTE: general knowledge, not checked in this session.]**
- **[NOTE]** NCSL's own summary text (17 states + D.C. through early voting and Election Day, plus 4 Election-Day-only) does not match its state table. The list above follows the state table. Rules on where and how to register (proof of residence, specific locations) vary; check the state's election office.
- **Deadlines in other states have passed.** Examples for Nov. 3, 2026:
  - **Texas:** Monday, **Oct. 5, 2026** (early voting Oct. 19–30). Source: https://www.sos.state.tx.us/about/newsreleases/2026/092826.shtml
  - **Arizona:** **Oct. 5, 2026**, 11:59 p.m. for online and in person; mailed forms postmarked by Oct. 5. Source: https://www.azfamily.com/2026/10/05/monday-is-deadline-register-vote-nov-general-election/
  - **Florida:** **Oct. 5, 2026** (has passed). The vote-by-mail request deadline was **Oct. 22, 2026, 5 p.m.** Source: https://www.wptv.com/news/political/elections-local/florida-election-2026-voter-registration-vote-by-mail-and-early-voting-deadlines-you-need-to-know
  - Look up any state: https://vote.gov and https://www.usa.gov/register-to-vote

## 3. Voter ID

- **36 states** request or require voters to show some form of ID at the polls. The other **14 states** (and D.C.) verify identity in other ways, such as a signature. As of October 2026.
  Sources: https://ballotpedia.org/Voter_identification_laws_by_state ; NCSL state table: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/voter-id
- NCSL's current table lists the 36 states as: AL, AK, AZ, AR, CO, CT, DE, FL, GA, ID, IN, IA, KS, KY, LA, MI, MS, MO, MT, NE, NH, NC, ND, OH, OK, RI, SC, SD, TN, TX, UT, VA, WA, WV, WI, WY.
  The 14 not listed: CA, HI, IL, ME, MD, MA, MN, NV, NJ, NM, NY, OR, PA, VT.
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/voter-id
- **Photo ID:** Per Ballotpedia (October 2026), **24 of the 36** states require or request **photo** ID (with exceptions), and **12** do not specifically require a photo.
  Source: https://ballotpedia.org/Voter_identification_laws_by_state
- **[UNCONFIRMED]** NCSL's current voter-ID page no longer shows its old strict/non-strict and photo/non-photo categories, so I could not confirm the photo-ID count from NCSL itself. NCSL's 2023–2024 categories gave a lower photo count (about 21–22 states, 10 of them strict). The difference may come from definitions or from changes in the law. A news article (MEAWW, Sept. 22, 2026) gives 36 / 24 / 12 and attributes it to NCSL. For the safest wording, say "36 states ask for ID; most of them ask for photo ID."
  Sources: https://news.meaww.com/which-voter-id-rules-have-changed-since-2022-heres-what-you-need-for-the-2026-midterms ; https://gigafact.org/fact-briefs/do-36-states-currently-have-some-sort-of-voter-id-law-in-effect (2023 baseline)
- **In most ID states, voters without ID still have options,** such as a provisional ballot or a signed affidavit. Details vary by state.
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/voter-id
- **Federal HAVA rule:** In every state, some **first-time voters who registered by mail** must show ID to cast a regular ballot (52 U.S.C. § 21083). If the ID number they gave at registration was not verified, they may need to show it.
  Source: https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-09-30_IF12033_c7c008e8e75daac3343ef34f550746e4d074de63.html (CRS, "The Help America Vote Act of 2002 (HAVA): An Overview")
  **[NOTE]** Not checked in this session: the detail that acceptable HAVA ID includes a current photo ID **or** a utility bill, bank statement, government check, paycheck or government document showing name and address, and that a voter without it can cast a provisional ballot. This comes from the statutory text (52 U.S.C. § 21083(b)). Verify before publishing.

## 4. Mail / absentee voting

- **All-mail elections (a ballot mailed to every registered voter): 8 states plus D.C.** They are California, Colorado, Hawaii, Nevada, Oregon, Utah, Vermont and Washington. Vermont does this for general elections only. NCSL updated Aug. 7, 2026.
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/table-18-states-with-all-mail-elections
- **Excuse required to vote absentee: 13 states** expected for November 2026. They are Alabama, Arkansas, Delaware, Indiana, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, New Hampshire, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas and West Virginia. **37 states plus D.C.** offer no-excuse absentee or all-mail voting. Connecticut moved to no-excuse absentee voting in 2026 (HB 5001). NCSL updated Aug. 26, 2026.
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/evolution-of-absentee-mail-voting-laws-2016-2026
  **[NOTE]** NCSL's separate "Table 2: Excuses to Vote Absentee" still says "Fifteen states…" and may not be updated. Use 13 and cite the evolution page. Some excuse states let voters 65 and older vote absentee without an excuse (e.g., Texas). **[Not individually verified.]**
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/table-2-excuses-to-vote-absentee
- **Every state** must send absentee ballots to military and overseas voters for federal elections (federal law).
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/table-2-excuses-to-vote-absentee
- **Return deadlines (receipt vs. postmark):** **35 states** require mailed ballots to be **received** by Election Day. **15 states plus D.C.** count mailed ballots that arrive after Election Day if they were **postmarked by** Election Day. Those 15 are AK, CA, IL, KS, MD, MA, MS, NV, NJ, NY, OR, TX, VA, WA and WV. The late-arrival window varies by state. NCSL updated Sept. 25, 2026.
  Source: https://www.ncsl.org/elections-and-campaigns/table-11-receipt-and-postmark-deadlines-for-absentee-mail-ballots
  **[NOTE]** NCSL mentions a Kansas court preliminary injunction connected to Kansas's grace period. Check Kansas's current status with the Kansas Secretary of State before naming it.
- **Supreme Court, Watson v. RNC (June 29, 2026):** The Court held that federal Election Day statutes do **not** stop states from counting ballots postmarked by Election Day and received afterward (Mississippi case; reversed the Fifth Circuit). So each state's own deadline still applies.
  Sources: https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/6/supreme-court-decides-watson-v-republican-national-committee-et-al ; NCSL Table 11 (above)
- **USPS postmark rule:** A USPS rule that **took effect Dec. 24, 2025** says "the postmark date does not necessarily indicate the first day that the Postal Service had possession of the mailpiece." The cause is fewer mail pickups and processing at regional facilities, especially in areas more than 50 miles from a processing center. A ballot dropped in the mail on Election Day may therefore be postmarked a day or more later. In postmark-deadline states, that could mean the ballot is rejected.
  Sources: https://www.klcc.org/politics-government/2026-01-03/usps-says-mail-in-ballots-might-not-get-postmark-on-same-day-theyre-dropped-off (Stateline) ; https://www.npr.org/2025/10/23/nx-s1-5582370/mail-in-ballot-postmark
- **Practical advice from the sources:**
  - USPS recommends mailing a completed ballot **at least one week before Election Day**.
  - To get a postmark dated the day you mail, ask for a **free manual (hand) postmark** at a post office retail counter.
  - Close to Election Day, election officials (e.g., California's Attorney General in 2025) advised using an **official drop box or vote center** instead of the mail.
  - Check the state's deadline (received by vs. postmarked by), and track the ballot where the state offers tracking.
  Sources: same as above

## 5. Section 203 of the Voting Rights Act (Spanish-language assistance)

- **The most recent determinations are from Dec. 8, 2021**, based on 2015–2019 ACS data. I found no 2026 determinations published as of Oct. 23, 2026. The Census Bureau page lists only the 2021, 2016, 2011 and 2002 determinations.
  Sources: https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2021/section-203-voting-rights-act.html ; https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial-census/about/voting-rights/voting-rights-determination-file.html
  **[NOTE]** Determinations come every five years, so a new set may come out around December 2026. Recheck before publishing anything after Election Day.
- **Total coverage (all languages):** **331 jurisdictions** (counties and minor civil divisions) plus 3 whole states. Covered jurisdictions hold **20,386,604 Hispanic** voting-age citizens.
  Source: https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2021/section-203-voting-rights-act.html
- **Spanish coverage:** Spanish assistance is required **statewide in California, Florida and Texas**, plus **232 political subdivisions in 26 states**. The 2016 determinations covered 214. In total, 30 states are covered in whole or in part for some language.
  Sources: https://advancingjustice-aajc.org/press-release/civil-rights-organizations-applaud-timely-release-acting-census-director-identifying ; https://www.lawyerscommittee.org/language-assistance-programs-play-vital-role-in-our-democracy/ ; https://narf.org/2021-voter-language-assistance-jurisdictions/
  **[NOTE]** The 232 / 26-state count comes from civil-rights groups summarizing the official Federal Register list. The Census press release itself does not break out Spanish.
- **What coverage requires:** Covered jurisdictions must provide "all information that is provided in English" **also in Spanish**. That includes voter registration materials, polling place notices, sample ballots, instructions, voter information pamphlets, and absentee and regular **ballots**. They must also give **oral assistance**, for example bilingual poll workers and staff who can answer questions. Translations must be accurate.
  Source: https://www.justice.gov/crt/language-minority-citizens
- **Coverage trigger:** A jurisdiction is covered when more than 10,000, or more than 5%, of its voting-age citizens belong to one language minority group, have limited English proficiency, and have a higher illiteracy rate than the national rate.
  Source: https://www.justice.gov/crt/language-minority-citizens
- **[NOTE, general]** Under federal law (VRA Section 208), any voter who needs help because of disability or inability to read or write can bring a helper of their choice, but not their employer or union representative. **[Not checked in this session; verify at https://www.justice.gov/crt before publishing.]**

## 6. Latino electorate size

- Pew Research Center projects about **39.0 million Latino (Hispanic) eligible voters** in 2026 (table: 38,950,000). That is **16% of all eligible voters** (about 249.75 million) and the **second-largest** group after White eligible voters.
  Source: https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/2026/09/10/the-u-s-electorates-changing-racial-and-ethnic-makeup/ (published Sept. 10, 2026)
- **Growth:** Latinos added **5.2 million** eligible voters between 2022 and 2026 (+16%), more than any other group. About **4 million** of the growth came from U.S.-born Latinos turning 18, and about **1 million** from naturalized immigrants.
  Source: same (growth breakdown confirmed via a secondary summary: https://www.fingerlakes1.com/2026/09/10/u-s-electorate-grows-more-diverse-ahead-of-2026-midterms-pew-finds/)
- **Other details:**
  - **77%** of Latino eligible voters were born in the U.S., and their median age is **39**.
  - States with the largest Latino shares of eligible voters: New Mexico (**43%**), California (**33%**), Texas (**32%**), Arizona (**25%**).
  Source: secondary summary of the Pew report (fingerlakes1 link above). **[NOTE: check these against the Pew page before publishing.]**
- **Method:** "Eligible voters" means U.S. citizens 18 and older living in the 50 states and D.C. The 2026 figures are Pew projections based on Census Bureau Vintage 2025 population estimates and the 2024 ACS.
  Source: Pew link above
